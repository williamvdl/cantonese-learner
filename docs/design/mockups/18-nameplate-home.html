Nameplate as a route home — v2

DES-18 is settled and unbuilt: the header nameplate becomes a live route to Home. This redraws mockup 18 on the v115 centred header. The first version drew all four options on the old left-aligned plate with both icons in the right corner — a layout no mockup had endorsed since 04, and one the app no longer has.

The nameplate is 廣東話 (gwong2 dung1 waa2) over Cantonese Learner, still a placeholder pending the naming decision. Type is not in scope here; position and behaviour are.

Centring changed the question, and added a second one. The plate now sits in a flex:1 slot between two fixed corners, so it no longer hugs its own text — which means where the tap zone ends is now a real decision, and a more consequential one than the decoration. That is part 1 below. The affordance question follows in part 2, and centring has moved its answer too.

Dashed = hit areas. The gold line is the true centre of the band.

Part 1 · Where the tap zone ends

Both frames behave identically and look identical at rest. They differ only in how much of the band is live. Turn the tap outlines on.

T-band · the button is the whole centre slot not recommended

Simplest markup — .header-title just becomes a <button>. But the live area is about 238×44px, and most of it is blank oxblood. On a sticky header present on every screen, a stray thumb on empty space during a scroll yanks you out of the lesson. An invisible target that large is a trap, not a convenience.

T-hug · the button hugs its text recommended

.header-title stays the centring container and the control shrinks to its text inside it — about 128×44px, still comfortably over the minimum in both directions. The bounds match what the eye thinks is tappable, which is also what makes a press state legible: it highlights the wordmark, not a band of empty colour.

This is the decision that actually matters here, and it did not exist before v115 — with a left-aligned shrink-to-fit plate, the button hugged its text whether you thought about it or not. Centring created the choice by giving the plate a slot wider than its contents.

Part 2 · The affordance

All four are T-hug, so the tap zone is identical across them. They differ only in how much the header admits the wordmark is a control.

N1 · No affordance recommended

Pixel-identical to v115 today. A centred wordmark flanked by two corner icons is a logo slot — the most established tap-to-go-home pattern there is. The left-aligned version read as a page heading, which is a much weaker cue; centring is what earns this option.

N2 · Press state only recommended with N1

Shown held down — at rest identical to N1. An opacity drop, which is exactly what .btn-icon--header already does for the two corner icons, so it introduces no new language. Confirms the tap on contact rather than advertising it beforehand.

N3 · Home glyph worse than in v1

Turn on the centre line. The glyph joins the plate, so the group centres and the wordmark itself sits left of true centre — the option defeats the change it is drawn on. It also puts a third icon in a band that already has two, and the nameplate stops being a nameplate and becomes a button with a label.

N4 · Underlined subtitle not recommended

An underline reads as a link, and this is the one element on screen that is not part of a document. It also lands under a 9px line tracked at 0.16em, where a hairline is closer to noise than signal.

Why there is no fifth option

There is no corner left for a home icon. The centred header has exactly two corner slots. The left holds the tone reference; the right holds the menu today and the settings cog after P6-2 and P6-4, per DES-11 — settings live behind the header corner regardless of feature count. So a dedicated home control has nowhere to go, and the nameplate is not one candidate among several: it is the only one. That is DES-18's own argument, and centring makes it structural rather than merely reasonable.

Recommendation

T-hug, N1 with N2's press state. The control hugs its text; nothing marks it at rest; an opacity drop confirms the tap. Centring is what makes N1 defensible where it was marginal before — a wordmark centred between two icons is read as a logo, and a logo goes home.

The argument against, unchanged and still worth holding: DES-18 exists because the nameplate is the escape hatch when the tab bar is hidden inside a topic (§3.10). An escape hatch that only works if you already know it is there is arguably not doing its job — and that is exactly the case it was approved to serve. §3.10 already says to judge the hidden state on device after shipping rather than on paper. If it reads as a dead end, N3 is the honest answer and this should be reopened rather than defended — accepting that N3 costs the centring and would need the glyph placed somewhere that does not.
