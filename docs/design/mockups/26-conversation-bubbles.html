Conversation bubbles — tone colour on a dark fill, and the redesign

Mockup 26. Issue 10 from device QA, which is two things: 10a, the tone colours are stripped from the learner's own lines, and 10b, the conversation could read more like a conversation. They are drawn together because the cheapest fix for 10a is also a redesign decision, so settling them separately would mean settling them twice.

10a is not a preference — it is measured, and it is worse than it looks

styles.css:674 forces every jyutping syllable on a right-side bubble to white with !important. That looks like an over-cautious override. It is not: every one of the six tone colours fails on both dark fills, and on the brand-orange fill the worst is 1.13:1, which is effectively invisible.

Option B — a second tone ramp for dark fills

Lighten each tone toward white until it clears the light-ground band. Computed rather than eyeballed. The result depends entirely on which fill you are lightening against, and that turns out to decide the whole question:

That second strip is the finding. Six near-identical off-whites carrying no information — a tone ramp that cannot be told apart is not a tone ramp, it is white with extra tokens. So option B cannot serve the topic conversation at all, only the checkpoint one. Which means B does not solve issue 10a; it solves half of it and leaves the brand-orange bubble needing a different answer anyway.

Option C — light the user bubble, keep one ramp recommended

Stop fighting the ground and change it. If the learner's bubble is a tint rather than a saturated fill, the existing tone ramp works unchanged — no second ramp, no new tokens, no divergence between what a colour means on the left and on the right.

A 3px edge on the outer side of each bubble, brand on yours and neutral on theirs, plus the speaker name in the small-caps meta treatment. This is the system's existing emphasis language (--edge-emph is already "louder than its neighbours" on next-up steps and checkpoint cards) rather than a new one. It restores the at-a-glance distinction the saturated fill was providing, without spending contrast to get it.

What C costs, stated plainly

The learner's line stops being the loudest thing on screen. That is a real loss and worth naming rather than glossing: a saturated bubble makes "this is yours" instant. Against it — §3.1 says the one filled element on a screen belongs to the action to take next, and in a conversation that is the play control or the Speak button, not a transcript line. The current design spends the screen's single loudest note on a label.

It is also more change than a colour swap. Every bubble state has to be re-checked against a light ground: --gap (the dashed fill-the-gap bubble), --correct, --wrong, .is-playing, and the speak-mode target line. Three of those are already light, so they will need their own separation from the newly-light user bubble rather than inheriting it by contrast.

Recommendation: C, built with C+'s edge and name treatment. It is the only option that answers issue 10a on both conversation types, it needs no new tokens, it keeps one meaning for one colour, and it removes the conditions that produced the v122 blank-play-button defect rather than patching around them. The 10b redesign then costs almost nothing extra, because the edge and the name label are the same edit.

If the loss of the saturated fill turns out to matter more on device than it does here, the fallback is B on the checkpoint conversation and today's white override on the topic one — accepting that the learner's tone colours exist in one place and not the other, and recording that as deliberate rather than leaving it to be re-discovered.

What gets recorded

Three rows if C is chosen. One for the fill decision, stating the rule as tone colour requires a light ground, so any surface carrying jyutping must be a tint and never a saturated fill — that is the durable form, and it prevents the same trap being set again on some future dark component. One for the speaker-distinction mechanism (edge plus name, not fill). One retiring the !important at styles.css:674, noting that it was correct given its constraints and that changing the ground is what made it unnecessary — a rule worth keeping, since the instinct on seeing an !important is to delete it rather than to ask what it was protecting.
