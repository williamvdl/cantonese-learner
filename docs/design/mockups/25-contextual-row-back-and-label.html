Contextual row — what back means, and what the count counts

Mockup 25. Two device-QA issues that live in the same twenty pixels and have to be decided together. Issue 4 is a plain defect. Issue 3 is a design question that reopens something the backlog settled deliberately, so it needs an argument rather than a fix.

The defect, first — it is not in dispute

renderContextRow() builds the meta slot as ${st.step} of ${st.total} · ${ctx.path.label}. st.step and st.total come from buildStageInfo() and are stage-scoped; ctx.path.label is the path. So a lesson two-thirds of the way through Eating and Shopping renders 2 of 5 · Beginner — a position inside the stage, labelled with the course. getPathContext()'s own comment says the row works in stage terms; only the label was left behind.

The count is right. The label is wrong. But it cannot be fixed on its own: the back control immediately to its left already says the stage name, so putting the stage in the meta slot too gives the same six words twice on one 360px row. That is why this mockup exists — issue 4's fix depends on what issue 3 decides.

What back does today

backAttr defaults to id="back-home-btn", whose handler is history.back(). The label is the stage name. So the control reads as a place and behaves as a direction, and the two only agree by luck:

This was a deliberate choice, not an oversight. BACKLOG.md records it: routing through history.back() keeps the on-screen back and the phone's hardware back in step. A control that always goes to one fixed destination necessarily disagrees with the hardware button, which always goes back one entry. Any breadcrumb buys honesty in the label by spending it in the relationship with the hardware key — that is the real trade, and it is what the three options below price differently.

The most honest and the most expensive. Both levels are named and both are tappable, so nothing is implied. But the back arrow has to go — there is no room for it beside two segments and a count — and with it goes the one glyph that says "this row is how you leave".

Look at the width. "Eating and Shopping" is not the longest stage name in the data, and this is 360px, which is a real phone. The segment already needs text-overflow: ellipsis to be safe, and a truncated breadcrumb segment is worse than no breadcrumb — it names a place using letters you can't read.

I would take B. It is the only option that makes the row state your position rather than merely offer an exit, it fixes issue 4 as a side effect rather than as a second edit, and it degrades correctly on a standalone topic with no special case. The hardware-key divergence it introduces is real but it is also ordinary — an in-app up control and a system back button pointing different ways is the pattern most apps already use, and the backlog note was written when nothing had tested whether the divergence actually confuses anyone.

The honest case for A is that it is one word, carries no risk at all, and the stepper directly beneath already shows your position within the stage more precisely than a breadcrumb would. If the row's job is "get me out of here", A does it and stops. Choose A if after seeing B on device the divergence bothers you — it is a strictly smaller change and nothing about B is hard to reach later.

C should not be built. Not because the idea is wrong — it is the most truthful of the three — but because it fails on the two things this mockup was drawn to test: it needs a truncating segment at a real phone width, and it needs a second shape for standalone topics, which is exactly what DES-25 settled against.

What gets recorded either way

Whichever wins, two register rows follow. One for the back semantics — and if it is B or C, it supersedes the backlog's checkpoint hub back target note rather than silently contradicting it, naming the hardware-key divergence as accepted rather than overlooked. One for the meta label, stating the rule as the count and its label must describe the same scope, which is the shape of the original defect and the thing a future edit could undo again.
