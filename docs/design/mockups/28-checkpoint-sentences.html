Checkpoint sentence review — listen-back, produce, and the combined form

Mockup 28, for the checkpoint sentence activity. Options B (listen, then say back) and C (English prompt, produce Cantonese) as standalone activities, then the combined form where the learner picks the method. Phone frames are 412px, Pixel 10's CSS viewport; chrome copied from the shipped markup per DESIGN_SYSTEM.md §8.

measured before designing

Four things checked against the real corpus, because each one changes what is buildable:

1. All 307 sentences have audio — every sid has a matching file in audio/sentences/, zero missing. B is viable today with no generation run.
2. Pools are 15–30 sentences per checkpoint stage (beginner 15–29, intermediate 16–30) — enough to sample 5–6 without repeats across several attempts.
3. 302 sentences carry a bd chunk breakdown — richer than assumed, and enough for a chunk-assembly variant later without new authoring.
4. No checkpoint stage has two sentences with the same English (0 of 15 stages) — so a C prompt always identifies exactly one target. That closes the ambiguous-prompt risk. It does not close the synonym risk — see below.

two constraints the design has to answer

17 sentences in checkpoint pools are under 4 characters — 請坐 (cing2 co5), 係呀！(hai6 aa3), 食飯喇！(sik6 faan6 laa3). DES-39 requires an exact match below four characters, with no leniency, because at that length one substitution is the whole word. Those 17 will false-reject far more often than the ~0% the leniency rules were tuned for. Proposal: the checkpoint pool filters to 4+ characters — it costs 17 of 302 sentences (5.6%) and keeps the grading honest. Filtering is one predicate in the pool builder, and the pools stay 15–30 either way.

C's synonym risk is real and not measurable from the data. "Thank you, goodbye!" has 多謝 (do1 ze6) as its target, but a learner who says 唔該 (m4 goi1) has produced defensible Cantonese and would be marked wrong — the corpus itself teaches both, and the note on that sentence explains the distinction. This is the same shape as the DES-37 prototype's permanent you were flatter than that verdict: correct input, told it is wrong. It is why C must never block, and why C's grading language differs from B's — see §4.

The tag question

Words is RECALL, Conversation is PRODUCE. A third activity needs a tag that isn't just a third synonym for "say things." Three candidates, and none is obviously right — this is a taste call worth making explicitly rather than defaulting:

Sentences SPEAK Names the mode. Honest, but flat — Conversation is also speaking.
Sentences RECALL → PRODUCE Accurate for the combined form (§3), but two words where the others have one.
Sentences SAY Parallel in length and register to RECALL/PRODUCE, and distinct from both.

I'd take SAY — it sits beside RECALL and PRODUCE as a third verb of the same weight, where SPEAK reads as a mode label and the arrow form breaks the pattern.

多謝 (do1 ze6) is for gifts and real kindness; 唔該 (m4 goi1) is for service and small favours. Both are thank-yous.

This frame is the whole argument for C's grading differing from B's. The learner produced good Cantonese. The app cannot tell that apart from an error — so it says neither (DES-44). No "correct", no "wrong", no "not wrong": just the sentence we had, what was heard, and the note. Every claim on this screen is one the app can actually stand behind, which is the DES-38 tone rule applied to a different uncertainty. Uses .speak-result-close (DES-42), already built for the "accepted but different" shape. The explanatory line is the sentence's own note field, authored on 302 of 302 sentences — no new content.

4 · Grading — graded, but never blocking

Per the brief: grade the same way Speak already does, but always allow continuing, and give the whole review a "mark complete." Three rules, and one thing deliberately not carried over from Words.

RuleWhy
Every result screen's "Next" is enabled, whatever the verdict. A mismatch changes the panel and the copy, never the ability to move on. The recogniser is wrong often enough that blocking on it would make the activity feel broken. This is DES-39's asymmetry applied to flow rather than to matching: a false reject that also traps you is much worse than one that doesn't.
An escape hatch on every prompt — "Show me the sentence" (B) / "Show me the answer" (C). Reveals the target and marks that item un-graded. Without it, a learner who genuinely cannot produce the sentence has no move except leaving. Marking it un-graded rather than failed keeps the tally honest without punishing the exit.
A "Mark reviewed" button completes the whole activity, exactly like Conversation's, and is available from the summary regardless of how the eight items went. Matches the brief, and matches the hub's own promise — the checkpoint says "Optional — do any, in any order," so an activity that could refuse to complete would contradict the screen it launched from.
No pass threshold, and no score out of eight on the hub card. The card shows done / not-done, like the other two. Words is a quiz and can defensibly score. This cannot: with a recogniser that produced a ~25% false-reject rate before DES-39's leniency, a stored "4 / 6" would be a permanent record of the recogniser's accuracy, not the learner's. Same reasoning as DES-37's refusal to show a score out of 100.

5 · Which eight, and what happens when they run out

The screens above say "Run eight more" without saying how the eight are chosen. Measured first, because the numbers decide the design: after the 4+ character filter, pools run 13–30 sentences, and 13 of 15 stages do not divide evenly by the run length. An awkward remainder is the normal case, not an edge case, so the rule has to handle it as its ordinary behaviour rather than as a special case bolted on.

No stage has a pool under 8, so every run fills. Only intermediate-s3 (16) and intermediate-s4 (24) divide cleanly.

A consequence of eight rather than six, recorded because it is a real trade. Five stages — beginner-s4, s5, s6, s8 (15 each) and s11 (13) — now get fewer than two full cycles. On those, run 2 already draws 2–3 items from the reshuffled next cycle and may repeat one from run 1; at six per run, repeats did not begin until run 3. This is a review activity and seeing a sentence twice is not a fault, but it is a different feel on the smallest stages and worth knowing before it is noticed in use.

The rule — a cursor over a ring, not a list of what's been seen

The obvious approach is storing which sids have been used. That is a growing array per checkpoint that needs pruning and invalidating, and it has to be persisted, migrated and reasoned about. The cheaper answer stores one integer.

Treat the pool as a continuous ring. Position p maps to cycle floor(p / poolSize) and index p % poolSize. Each cycle's order is a seeded shuffle keyed on cpId + cycle — deterministic, so the same cycle always produces the same order and the cursor alone reconstructs what has been seen, with nothing else stored. A run takes positions cursor … cursor+7 and advances the cursor by eight.

Crossing a cycle boundary needs no special case. If three positions remain in cycle 2, the run takes those three plus the first three of cycle 3's freshly-shuffled order, and the cursor lands mid-cycle-3. Always exactly six, always unseen-first, and the remainder problem disappears rather than being handled.

Worked against the real beginner-s1 pool — 25 sentences across five topics (pronouns 8, greetings 5, yesno 3, numbers 4, phrases 5). Each box is one position in the ring; colour is the run that consumes it.

Run 4 is the interesting one: 1 position left in cycle 0, topped up with the first 7 of cycle 1. Eight items, none of them repeats, and the cursor simply carries on. Run 5 continues from position 7 of cycle 1 — no repeat of run 4's top-up, because the cursor moved past them.

Ordering within a cycle: round-robin by topic, shuffled within each. A pure shuffle of beginner-s1's pool can hand back four sentences from pronouns (which contributes 8 of the 25) and one each from two others — a lumpy sample of a stage that is meant to be reviewed as a whole. Interleaving across topics first, then shuffling within each topic and shuffling the topic order per cycle, gives spread and variety at no extra storage.

Where the integer lives: pathProgress[pathKey]['cp:<cpId>:sentences:cursor']. Verified against migratePathProgressIfNeeded() — it returns early on any key starting with cp:, so a numeric value there is safe from the legacy-key rewrite that would otherwise mangle it. Persisted by the existing savePathProgress(). No new storage surface, no schema change.

Two caveats, recorded rather than discovered later

Near-repeats can cross a boundary. A reshuffle can place an item late in cycle N and early in cycle N+1, so run 5's top-up could in principle include something from run 4. Mitigable by seeding cycle N+1's shuffle to push the previous cycle's tail to the back, but that is real complexity for a rare, mild artefact — not proposed.

Content changes make "seen" approximate. Adding or editing sentences changes poolSize, which shifts the position→item mapping, so a cursor stored under the old size no longer means quite what it meant. Worst case is one early repeat after a content change. Acceptable; the alternative is versioning the pool, which costs more than the problem.

Running out — there is no wall

The ring never exhausts; it cycles. What is worth deciding is whether the learner is told when they have been through everything once.

Only appears on the run that completes a cycle; every other run shows the plain summary from §4 with "Run eight more". No confirmation gate — asking "are you sure you want to practise more?" is friction on exactly the thing the learner just asked for.

Why this composes with completion

The cursor is independent of the done flag. cp:<id>:sentences (boolean, "reviewed") and cp:<id>:sentences:cursor (integer, "how far through") are separate keys that never read each other.

That is what makes coming back work, and it falls out of the design rather than needing a feature: a learner can mark the activity reviewed after one run, have the checkpoint show ✓ Done, and return three weeks later to six sentences they have not seen. The hub is unchanged, the completion is unchanged, and the content is new.

Nothing resets the cursor — not marking complete, not un-marking it, not finishing the checkpoint. The only thing that advances it is running sentences, which is the only thing that should.

6 · Decisions — all settled 2026-08-30

All seven answered by William and recorded as DES-44 (activity shape) and DES-45 (sampling). The "my lean" column is kept as the reasoning that was offered, with the actual decision marked — two of them went against my lean and the difference is worth being able to find later.

QuestionOptionsDecision
Standalone or combined? Is this one hub card with a mode picker, or would B and C ever be two separate cards? One card with the picker (mocked in §4) · two cards · one card, mode remembered from last time and changeable ✓ One card with the picker. Two cards would make a three-activity checkpoint into a four-activity one and dilute the "recall → produce" shape.
How does C treat a synonym? The "not wrong" panel in §3 is only honest if the app can tell a synonym from an error — and it can't, without alternates in the data. (a) Show that panel for every C mismatch, generously · (b) Author an alt array on sentences where a synonym is plausible · (c) Use the DES-39 leniency unchanged and accept some false rejects ✓ (d) — state no verdict at all, a fourth option added after William asked for the question to be explained. (a) makes a claim the app cannot verify and would excuse genuine errors too; (c) tells a learner who produced defensible Cantonese that they were wrong. Instead show the target, show what was heard, show the sentence's own note, and assert nothing about correctness — the DES-38 instinct applied to a different uncertainty. (b) stays the better long-term answer if specific sentences prove to attract the same valid alternative.
Six items, or a different count? 5 · 6 · 8 · all of them ✓ Eight — William's call, against my lean of six. Every stage still fills a run, at the cost of earlier repeats on the five smallest stages (see §5).
Filter out the 17 short sentences? Yes, pool is 4+ characters · No, accept the false rejects · Yes, but show them un-graded ✓ Yes, filter. 5.6% of the corpus for grading that stays trustworthy.

Completing a stage's whole sentence pool is a real accomplishment and the app has nowhere else that says so. No confirmation gate — that is friction on the thing the learner just asked for.
Cursor or seen-list? §5 proposes one integer per checkpoint; the alternative is storing which sentences have actually been shown. One integer instead of a growing array, no pruning or invalidation, and the remainder case stops being a special case. The two caveats in §5 are the price and both are mild. "No tracking" is the honest cheap option but produces immediate repeats, which is the thing the question was asked to avoid.
