Tier treatment at two tiers and three

Revised on the assumption you gave: tier 2 becomes the norm rather than the exception once the intermediate vocabulary lands, and tier 3 arrives with Advanced. That changes the problem. Mockup 23 was drawn against 8 multi-tier topics out of 42 and quietly optimised for a control that mostly is not there. It should be drawn against a topic that has three tiers and a learner sitting in the middle one. The nameplate reads 廣東話 (gwong2 dung1 waa2).

0 · The five states, and the one that decides it

You asked the right question — what does tier 2 look like, with and without a tier 3? Writing the matrix out is what settles this, because four of the five states are easy and the fifth breaks one of the options outright.

Tier 2 of 3 is the state that decides the design, and it is the state that will be most common once Advanced exists — the middle rung is where most learners sit for longest. Your instinct about progressing one at a time is right and it is what the ladder should do, but the middle rung needs both directions, and the moment a line carries two offers it is a chooser again, which is what option C was chosen to avoid.

The way out is that C and a pager are the same control at different sizes. C is not really "state plus an offer" — it is a ladder whose two-tier case happens to have only one rung to show. Generalise it and the two-tier states come out exactly as drawn in mockup 23, unchanged. That is option D below, and it is the same thing you already picked, extended rather than replaced.

What the matrix shows, reading down. The ladder holds its shape at every state and never wraps — at three tiers the two rungs shorten to bare numbers, which they can afford because the state text beside them already says "Tier 2 of 3", so the numbers are unambiguous. The chip and marker rows both break at three tiers: three chips plus the word count exceed 312px, so the count is forced onto its own line and the block grows from one row to two — the single row under the title, which was the whole point of the placement, stops being one row exactly when tiers become common.

And the structural objection gets worse, not better, at three. A two-up chooser above a three-up chooser reads as a hierarchy that does not exist. A three-up chooser above a three-up chooser reads as the same control repeated, which is the strongest possible version of what you flagged. A and B were already the weaker options; three tiers is what rules them out rather than merely ranking them.

3 · Why the ladder, and what it costs

D is C, generalised — not a replacement for it. At two tiers the two produce identical markup: state text on the left, one named offer on the right. Everything you liked about C is preserved, and the only thing that changes is that the offer becomes a list of up to two rather than exactly one. If tier 3 never arrives, D and C are the same screen forever.

The one asymmetry worth deciding deliberately: down is muted, up is brand. The ladder has a direction — the learner is climbing it — so the upward rung reads as the offer and the downward one as a way back. That also keeps exactly one brand-coloured interactive element above the subtabs, which was the open question from mockup 23 and is answered here by the shape rather than by picking a colour. If you would rather both rungs were equal weight, say so: it is one declaration, but it changes the control from a ladder into a pager, and the middle state then has no default.

What it costs. One new component, .ladder / .rung, in place of .round-selector / .round-label — a net reduction. The lookup helper for "which path owns this tier of this topic" is needed either way and now returns a small list rather than a single answer, which the cross-reference card renders as one row per entry. No new selected-state language is invented, because as with C there is no selected state — there is a stated position and one or two offers.

The limit, stated rather than buried. The ladder assumes tiers are ordered and adjacent — that from tier 2 the only sensible moves are to 1 and 3. That is true of a difficulty scale and it is what your description of the roadmap implies. It would stop being true if tiers ever became parallel variants rather than depths, in which case a chooser is correct and A returns. Worth a line in the register as the condition under which this decision should be reopened.
